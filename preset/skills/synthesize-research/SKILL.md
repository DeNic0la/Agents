# Synthesize research

Turn user research from several sources into structured findings and recommendations.

## 1. Gather inputs

Accept any mix of pasted notes, transcripts, survey exports and files. If connected, also search the knowledge base for research documents, the feedback or support tool for tickets and feature requests, product analytics for behavioral data, and meeting transcripts for recorded interviews.

Ask the user:

- What kind of research? Interviews, surveys, usability tests, analytics, support tickets, sales call notes.
- How many sources or participants?
- Which question or hypothesis are they investigating?
- Which decision will it inform?

## 2. Process each source

Extract:

- **Observations:** what users said, did or experienced.
- **Quotes:** verbatim, attributed to participant type ("enterprise admin, 200-person team"), never by name.
- **Behaviors:** what they actually did, as opposed to what they said they do.
- **Pain points:** frustrations, workarounds, unmet needs. A workaround is an unmet need in disguise.
- **Positive signals:** what works, moments of delight.
- **Context:** segment, use case, experience level.

## 3. Find themes

Group observations into themes, count how many participants or sources support each, and assess impact. Note contradictions and surprises.

Prioritize:

- high frequency, high impact: top findings
- low frequency, high impact: important for a segment
- high frequency, low impact: quality-of-life improvements
- low frequency, low impact: note and deprioritize

Read [references/methods.md](references/methods.md) when doing thematic analysis or affinity mapping, analyzing interview notes or survey data, combining qualitative and quantitative evidence, building personas, or sizing opportunities.

## 4. Write the synthesis

1. **Overview:** methods, number of participants or sources, research questions, timeframe.
2. **Key findings (5-8),** ordered by frequency x impact. Each has a one-sentence finding, evidence with source attribution, frequency, impact, and confidence: high (strong evidence), medium (suggestive), low (early signal). Each finding must stand on its own.
3. **Segments or personas,** if distinct ones emerge: description, behaviors, needs, size if known.
4. **Opportunity areas:** unmet or underserved needs, where current solutions fall short, ranked by impact.
5. **Recommendations:** specific actions tied to findings, ranked by impact and feasibility. "Add a progress indicator to setup", not "improve onboarding".
6. **Open questions:** what the research didn't answer, and suggested follow-up methods.

## 5. Review and extend

Ask whether any finding needs more detail or different framing. Offer personas, an opportunity map, a presentation, a follow-up research plan, or roadmap implications (the `roadmap-update` skill applies them).

## Rules

- Let the data speak. Don't force findings into a predetermined story.
- Behavior beats stated preference.
- A finding from 2 interviews is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. Say so.
- Contradictions are signal; they often reveal distinct segments.
- 5-8 strong findings beat 20 weak ones.
