# Brainstorming frameworks and techniques

Pull one in when it moves the conversation. Never run every conversation through every framework.

## Ideation techniques

- **Constraint removal:** what would you build with no technical, budget or political constraints? Then work back to what's feasible.
- **Analogies:** how does another industry solve this? What can we steal?
- **Inversion:** how would we make this worse? Reverse each answer.
- **Decomposition:** split into subproblems, solve each, then combine.
- **Hat-switching:** how would a power user solve it? A brand-new user? An admin? Someone who hates our product?

## How Might We

"How might we [desired outcome] for [user] without [constraint]?"

- Too broad: "How might we improve onboarding?"
- Too narrow: "How might we add a tooltip to step 3?" (that's a solution)
- Right: "How might we help new users reach their first success within 10 minutes?"

Write 5-10 HMW questions per problem statement. Each reframing opens a different solution space.

## Jobs to Be Done

"When [situation], I want to [motivation] so I can [expected outcome]."

- The job stays stable while solutions change: sharing updates with colleagues went from memos to email to chat to shared docs.
- Functional jobs are easiest to spot. Emotional jobs (feel confident) and social jobs (be seen as a leader) are often stronger.
- Ask "what did they fire to hire our product?" to reveal the real competitive set.

## Opportunity solution tree

```
Desired outcome
├── Opportunity A (user need or pain)
│   ├── Solution A1
│   │   ├── Experiment
│   │   └── Experiment
│   └── Solution A2
│       └── Experiment
└── Opportunity B
    ├── Solution B1
    └── Solution B2
```

- Opportunities come from research, not imagination. Each traces back to evidence.
- One solution per opportunity means you haven't explored enough.
- Several experiments per solution; find the cheapest test before building.
- Keep updating the tree as you learn.

## First principles

1. State the problem or assumption.
2. Break it into fundamental components and constraints.
3. Question each: law of physics, or convention?
4. Rebuild from the fundamental truths only.

Use it when the team is stuck in incremental thinking, when everyone says "that's just how it works", or when the category hasn't been rethought in years.

## SCAMPER

Seven lenses on an existing product or process:

- **Substitute:** replace a component, or have a different user do a step.
- **Combine:** merge two features, workflows or roles.
- **Adapt:** borrow from another product or industry.
- **Modify:** 10x bigger, smaller, faster.
- **Put to other use:** serve a different user or use case.
- **Eliminate:** remove it entirely. Would anyone notice?
- **Reverse:** do the opposite, flip the sequence, invert the default.

## OODA loop

Observe, orient, decide, act, and cycle faster than the competition.

1. **Observe:** gather raw signals widely: usage, feedback, competitor moves, market shifts, tickets.
2. **Orient:** make sense of them. The critical step. Are you seeing what's there or what you expect?
3. **Decide:** a hypothesis to test, sized to what you know. Small bets when unsure.
4. **Act:** ship, run the experiment, then observe again.

Use it when the team over-deliberates, when competitive timing matters, when a session circles without converging (it makes the decision reversible), or for "given what we see in the market, how should we re-orient?" Most teams get stuck in orient; the team that cycles fastest learns fastest.

## Reverse brainstorming

1. Invert: "How could we make onboarding as confusing as possible?"
2. List everything that would make it worse: more steps, jargon, hidden buttons, no feedback.
3. Reverse each into a fix.
4. Pick the most promising.

It works because people are better at spotting what's wrong than imagining what's right.
