# Competitive analysis frameworks

## Competitive set

Define competitors at four levels:

- **Direct:** same problem, same users, same approach. They show up in your deals and in review-site matchups.
- **Indirect:** same problem, different approach (spreadsheets vs. a project tool). Include non-consumption: doing nothing or a manual process.
- **Adjacent:** don't compete today but could. Similar technology, customers or distribution; big platforms that could add your feature; niche startups that could grow into your core market.
- **Substitutes:** a different way to meet the need. Hiring a person, a general-purpose tool (Excel, email), outsourcing.

### Landscape map

Place competitors on two axes that reveal strategic differences in your market:

- breadth vs. depth (suite vs. point solution)
- SMB vs. enterprise
- self-serve vs. sales-led
- simple vs. powerful
- horizontal vs. vertical

### Monitoring

Track launches and changelogs, pricing changes, funding and acquisitions, key hires and job postings, your wins and losses against them, analyst and review coverage, partnerships.

## Feature comparison matrix

1. Group features into capability areas the way buyers evaluate, not by your architecture.
2. List specific capabilities under each area.
3. Rate each competitor on a consistent scale.

**Simple scale (default):** Strong (market-leading), Adequate (works, not differentiated), Weak (exists, real gaps), Absent.

**Detailed scale (deep dives):** 5 best-in-class, 4 strong, 3 adequate, 2 limited, 1 minimal or early beta, 0 absent.

```
| Capability area | Our product | Competitor A | Competitor B |
|-----------------|-------------|--------------|--------------|
| [Area 1]        |             |              |              |
|   [Feature 1]   | Strong      | Adequate     | Absent       |
|   [Feature 2]   | Adequate    | Strong       | Weak         |
```

"How well does it do X" beats "has X". Add why each capability area matters to buyers. Comparisons go stale fast; update them.

## Positioning analysis

**Positioning statement:** For [target customer] who [need], [Product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], [Product] [differentiator].

Sources: homepage headline, app store and review-site descriptions, sales decks shared by prospects, analyst material, earnings calls.

**Message architecture:**

1. Category they claim (CRM, project management)
2. Differentiator within it (AI-powered, all-in-one, developer-first)
3. Value proposition, the promised outcome
4. Proof points (logos, metrics, awards, case studies)

**Look for:** unclaimed positions buyers care about, crowded claims that have lost meaning, emerging positions driven by market change, claims a competitor can't fully deliver on.

## Win/loss analysis

The most actionable competitive intelligence.

**Sources:** CRM notes (fast but biased), customer interviews soon after the decision (best), churn surveys and exit interviews, prospect surveys for lost deals.

**Win questions:** What problem were you solving? What else did you evaluate? Why us? What almost made you choose someone else? What would make you reconsider?

**Loss questions:** What problem were you solving? What did you choose, and why? Where did we fall short? What could we have done differently? Would you reconsider us, and when?

**Analysis:** track reasons over time; segment by deal type (enterprise vs. SMB, new vs. expansion, vertical); find the top 3-5 reasons each way; separate product reasons from non-product ones (pricing, brand, relationship, timing); compute win rate per competitor.

**Common patterns:** a dealbreaker feature gap, integration with tools the buyer already uses, a better-fitting pricing model, incumbent switching cost, sales execution (demo, speed, case studies), brand and perceived safety.

## Market trends

**Sources:** analyst reports, VC investment themes, conference topics, technology shifts, regulation, changing customer expectations, talent movement.

For each trend: what is changing, why now, who it affects, the timeline (now, 1-2 years, 3-5 years), what it means for us, how competitors respond.

**Signal vs. noise:** signals have behavioral data, growing investment, regulation or customer demand behind them. Noise is hype without customer traction. Test every trend against your own customers.

**Responses:** lead (invest early, high risk and reward), fast follow (move once demand shows), monitor (set triggers for acting), ignore (decide explicitly and write down why). The choice depends on your position, customers, resources and the trend's speed.
